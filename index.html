Homes of the Future: Designing Tomorrow’s Living Spaces

What could home life be like in 50–100 years? Research ideas being developed today, imagine future challenges, and design your own home. Explain how at least three special features would help the people who live there.

📌 What to Hand In

  • Report — 50%: Describe your future world, research findings, and home design. Explain at least three special features. Include a conclusion and source list.
  • Visual design — 25%: Show your home through a labelled sketch, a plan showing rooms, a 3D model, or a digital design.
  • Presentation — 25%: Give a 5–10 minute talk. Use your design to explain your ideas and answer questions.
  • Your own work: Make an original design. Give credit for borrowed facts, ideas, images, and quotations.

🏡 Choose Your Design Format

  • Sketch or plan: Draw the home, label the features, and show how the spaces connect. Add sizes or a simple scale where useful.
  • Physical 3D model: Build a model using materials you can work with. Add labels or a key to explain the features.
  • Digital design: Use slides with images, a digital mood board (a collection of design images and notes), or a video tour.
  • Your design must show your ideas clearly. Simple tools are enough if your explanations are clear.

🎯 Explain Your Home

  • Year and place: Choose a year 50–100 years from now and a real, imagined, or virtual location.
  • Future challenges: Explain what might change, such as the climate, population, space, energy, or water supply.
  • At least three features: For each one, explain what it is, how it might work, and which challenge it could solve.
  • Research and imagination: Show which ideas are based on current research and which are your own predictions.

🔎 Four Questions to Research

1. Physical or virtual?

Will people live mainly in physical homes, or spend more time in virtual spaces? If your home includes virtual reality, where will people sleep, eat, and keep their bodies safe?

2. Materials and building

What could the home be made of? Research ideas such as 3D printing, materials that repair themselves, or building with living materials. Explain how your choice could help.

3. Location and shape

Could homes float on water, be built underground, move to new places, or exist on another planet? Why might people need a different location or shape?

4. Daily life

How would people get energy, water, and food? Could AI manage the home? How might furniture change? Explain what a normal day would look like.

Research tip: Start with 3–4 reliable sources as a useful target. Try research organisations, original project descriptions, and well-known science or architecture publications. Check who wrote each source and when. A design idea being tested today is not a promise that it will be common in the future.

🧩 Connect the Problem to the Design

For each feature, follow this pattern: Future challenge → Design feature → How it could work → Benefit → Possible limit. A possible limit is something that might make the idea difficult to use.

Imagined example: Summers might become hotter. My home could have a roof garden to provide shade. Plants might help keep the rooms cooler, but they would need enough water. I would collect rainwater to help water them. This is a design idea to research, not proof that it will work everywhere.

Report plan: Title and your name → Introduction → Future year, location, and challenges → Research under the four questions → Your design and at least three features → Daily life → Conclusion → Source list.

Useful check: Does every special feature solve a clear problem? Explain the connection. Avoid adding technology only because it looks exciting.

💡 Useful Language: Predictions and Design Ideas

Use might, may, and could for possibilities. Use would to explain life in your imagined design. Give a reason for each prediction.

In [year], people might... This home could be built... It might be powered by... This feature would help because... If..., the home would... One possible problem is...

Example: If cities had less space, homes could use furniture that folds away. This would allow one room to have several uses.

Separate evidence from prediction: “According to [source], researchers are testing...” describes research you have checked. “In my design, this technology might...” introduces your prediction.

Past guesses, when they fit: Imagine someone in the future looking at an old home. “It might have been built to...” suggests a possible reason. “It must have been used for...” expresses a strong belief based on evidence. Use these forms only when talking about the past.

🛡️ Academic Honesty: Show What Is Yours and What Comes from a Source

Academic honesty means being clear about whose words, ideas, and work you use. Your project should show your understanding. Give credit for borrowed ideas, facts, exact words, pictures, charts, and videos. A source note tells the reader where information came from.

A key rule: Changing a few words, translating a text, or turning a quotation into reported speech does not make the idea yours. You still need to name the source. A source list at the end is not enough on its own: add a source note beside the borrowed information too.

📚 Keep useful source notes
  • When you research, save the author or organisation, title, date, link, and any useful page number or video timestamp.
  • Keep exact quotations in quotation marks in your notes. Put your own notes beside them so you can tell them apart later.
  • Use a short source note in the report, such as (Author, year). For an organisation's work, use its name. If no date is given, use n.d. (“no date”). Follow any format your teacher gives you.
  • At the end, list full source details. A simple web-source pattern is: Author or organisation. (Year or n.d.). Title. Website name. URL.
  • Match every source note to an entry in your source list. Use sources you have actually read or watched. Never invent an author, quotation, date, or link.
🖼️ Pictures, charts, videos, and help from others
  • Add credit beside a borrowed image, chart, or clip, and include the full source in your source list. For example: Image: [creator], [website], [year].
  • If you make a chart using someone else's data, write Chart by me; data from [source] ([year]).
  • Crediting an image does not automatically give you permission to reuse it. Use your own images or material that allows reuse, and follow its conditions.
  • For your own photos or drawings, you can write Photo/drawing by me. You do not need a research source for your own opinions or widely known facts.
  • Follow your teacher's rules about outside help and AI. If help is allowed, explain what help you used. Check facts against real sources and make sure you understand and can explain your final work.

✅ Before you hand in: Are exact words in quotation marks? Are paraphrases truly in your own words? Are borrowed ideas and visuals credited beside them? Does every source note match the source list? Can you explain what you wrote?
